Fifty songs, zero hits, one actual experiment

Five article sets, five writing ideas, two generations each: a test matrix disguised as a jukebox. The multiplication was the easy part; comparing the results fairly was the actual work.

We wanted to compare writing ideas rather than crown the first prompt that produced a song-shaped object. The plan was a matrix: five representative article sets, five writing ideas and two independent generations for every cell. That is 5 × 5 × 2 = 50 songs. The multiplication is the easy part. The difficult part is remembering that a matrix on a ticket is not a folder full of listening evidence.

What happened

The story-song ticket says the selected material should come from two to four top stories, while the writing format may vary between verses, refrains and short narrative passages. It also specifies what each result must retain: article-set ID, writing-idea ID, generation number, rendered lyrics, audio asset, duration and relevant prompt or model metadata.

The 22 August diary records this as a defined evaluation, and the audio source trail routes execution 1523 in session 20260822_202002_441297 to the planned 50-song listening corpus. Those records establish the test design and its route. They do not give us permission to turn the planned corpus into completed results.

What the evidence changed

The matrix forces the comparison to answer a useful question. Does a writing idea work across different article mixes, and does it survive a second generation? Lyrics let us inspect structure and whether the source stories remain recognisable. Audio lets us hear whether the words fit a song. Duration tells us whether the format has quietly become a fixed short-form template again. Prompt and model metadata explain which conditions produced a result.

Without those fields, "idea four sounded best" would be a memory, not a finding. With them, a reviewer can compare like with like. The two independent generations matter because one lucky output can flatter a weak prompt. We were trying to choose a repeatable writing approach, not find a single charming accident. The article-set ID also keeps the comparison honest: a prompt may look good because one set is unusually easy to turn into lyrics. Without that label, the winning idea could simply be the prompt that met the friendliest stories.

Where we left it

The records support a 50-cell test plan and identify the session route for it. They do not establish that 50 songs were generated, listened to or accepted. No duration table, audio inventory or completed review is supplied by the evidence read for this post. We therefore leave the choice of production prompt open.

That is less exciting than announcing a winner, but it is much more useful. A test plan can be counted before it is run. Fifty imaginary songs remain imaginary, even when the spreadsheet would have looked excellent on paper.
