August's greatest hits: locks that held and logins that didn't

Two kinds of trouble arrived at once that month: ComfyUI refusing to be interrupted, and the profile-switch path rejecting our credentials. Keeping them as separate diagnoses was the only way through.

August gave us two kinds of operational trouble at once: ComfyUI did not want to be interrupted, and the profile-switch path did not always accept us as authenticated. The useful part of the story is that these were recorded as separate symptoms. It is tempting to put every red line under one grand root cause. The evidence did not earn that confidence.

What happened

Execution 2240 failed at Assert music ComfyUI ready after roughly twenty minutes. The 25 August diary records lock-file investigation and cleanup, followed by work on autosave failures. The audio path also exposed HTTP 401 responses around the profile-switch and credential route. The raw source trail identifies executions 2240, 2245 and 2247, with 2247 recorded as a retry of 2245.

Those details describe a messy evening, not one indivisible failure. A lock owner concerns shared ComfyUI state. HTTP 401 means the request was rejected for authentication. They can appear in the same recovery session without sharing a cause.

What the evidence changed

The recorded recovery work included checking the graph, clearing stale lock files, rotating the ComfyUI profile authentication, and disentangling n8n credential draft state from the published display. The diary also records a separate wiring correction: audio finishing was happening before imaging because of a dropdown connection, not because the lock or the 401 had magically swallowed the media branch.

That separation improved the diagnosis. A lock message can tell us that a process owns a protected resource. It does not tell us that the owner is stale. A 401 can tell us that authentication failed. It does not tell us that the ComfyUI process is unhealthy. We kept the terms attached to their observed boundaries instead of turning them into a single story about "the profile".

That separation gave us one more practical rule: recovery output should never silently replace the original failure record. On 26 August, the daily note says the last two runs failed again and the workflow returned to debugging. One clean run therefore remained a checkpoint, not a cure.

Where we left it

August established a recovery pattern and a warning. Lock ownership, profile authentication and node wiring each needed their own checks. Some wiring and recovery work was recorded, and one clean run followed. The next day's failures prevented a claim of permanent resolution.

We did not discover a single villain hiding behind every error. We found several small systems, each capable of making a convincing mess. That is less satisfying than a master diagnosis, but it is a better incident record and a safer basis for the next repair. It also leaves the next check obvious.
