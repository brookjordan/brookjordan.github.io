PodCast Builder · devlog

Written retrospectively from records covering 16 to 19 September 2026.

The longer-sequence experiment had two different bets hiding inside one accounting table. One arm asked a single prompt and one long render to carry the whole action. The other split the action into two continuous sections, rendered each section, then joined them. Both aimed at longer podcast videos, but they put continuity in different places: inside one model pass, or at the join between two passes.

What happened

PCB-012 froze five real podcast cases before rendering: atmospheric drift, mercury ripples, fog, mechanical breathing and a hologram pulse. Its planned matrix was three renders per case: one single-prompt render, then two parts for the joined arm. That means 15 renders and 10 finished videos, calculated as five single videos plus five joined videos. The source case packet records the motion plans and matched start/end image pairs; it does not record completed outcomes for the matrix.

The experiment was designed around a longer continuous sequence. The single-prompt arm combined the motion sections without requesting a hard cut. The two-part arm described a continuous sequence in two sections, with the joined result expected to be longer than the single render. The distinction matters because a join is not a free extension. It creates a new boundary that must itself be reviewed.

What the evidence changed

A single long clip tests whether the model can preserve the scene and action across one extended interpolation. Two joined clips test whether two shorter decisions can be assembled without an obvious discontinuity. The second arm may reduce the length of each interpolation arc, but it moves some risk into editing: timing, visual state at the join and prompt continuity become part of the acceptance question.

The source ticket required full-speed review of both finished-video types for continuity, unwanted cuts, smear, freezing, guide preservation and prompt adherence. It also required the source clips to remain as evidence. Those requirements prevent the accounting totals from being mistaken for performance results. Ten finished outputs was the planned shape, not a claim that ten good videos existed.

Where we left it

The recorded status was preparatory rather than conclusive. The five cases were frozen and the ten source images were verified, while the 15-render matrix still awaited duration evidence. No result in the available records establishes that the matrix finished, that joins were better, or that every joined video exceeded its single counterpart.

We therefore kept the two arms as different bets. A single long clip concentrates continuity risk in one render. Two joined clips distribute it across two renders and a join. The next defensible step was to account for every render and inspect every finished video at full speed. Until that happened, the architecture comparison remained a design for an experiment, not a verdict.
