Shokz Auto-Fill watches for your player. The moment it appears, it pulls a brand-new random set of songs from your own Navidrome server, keeps the two newest lists, and ejects the drive for you.

Install it See how it works
No app to open Nothing to sync Nothing leaves your Mac

It hears the drive arrive

A LaunchAgent watches /Volumes. Plug the player in and the job starts on its own — no menu bar app, no window, no clicking.

It fetches a fresh set

Fifty songs by default, chosen at random from everything on your server, written into a new numbered folder on the player.

It tidies up and ejects

The two newest lists stay; older ones are cleared out. Then the drive is ejected, so you can unplug it and go.

The whole loop

Three moves, none of them yours

This is the entire user experience. There is no fourth step, and no interface to learn.

1

You plug the player in

Any player that mounts as a USB drive under /Volumes — a Shokz OpenSwim, an old iPod in disk mode, a cheap clip-on MP3 player. The job waits two seconds for macOS to finish mounting, then looks for the volume by name.

2

It fills the drive

It asks your Navidrome server for random songs, downloads them into a new folder (auto-list/1, auto-list/2, …), and numbers every track so the player plays them in the order you got them.

3

It ejects and you go

Old lists beyond the two newest are removed, the filesystem is synced, and the volume is ejected with diskutil. Unplug it and put it on. Tomorrow's plug-in is a different fifty songs.

The gear

Any player that mounts as a drive

It was written for a Shokz OpenSwim, and it works with the OpenSwim Pro, older Aftershokz players, most clip-on MP3 players, and anything else Finder shows you as a removable volume.

The only requirement is that the player appears as a folder under /Volumes and is formatted FAT32 or exFAT. If you can drag an MP3 onto it in Finder and the player plays it, this tool can fill it.

No phone, no companion app, no account, no proprietary transfer software. The player just has to look like a USB stick — which, to your Mac, is exactly what it is.

Why this is a project and not a one-liner

The interesting part is the permission

Copying files onto a USB stick is trivial. Doing it from a background job on modern macOS is not.

macOS gates removable volumes behind a privacy service. When a background job runs /bin/bash, bash is the responsible process — and /bin/bash is an Apple platform binary. macOS will not grant those a permission, and cannot even ask. The refusal is permanent and completely silent: every mkdir on the drive fails with Operation not permitted, and nothing tells you why.

The fix is to run the job under a shell that is not a platform binary, so macOS will put up a dialog and then grant it. That is the entire reason install.command installs a Homebrew bash, and the reason it walks you through one Allow prompt.

The awkward questions

Where your password goes, and where it doesn't

Nothing leaves your Mac

Your music lives on your Navidrome server. This tool talks to that server and to the drive in front of you. There is no account, no telemetry, no analytics, no update check, and no third party anywhere in the path. The whole thing is a shell script you can read in one sitting.

Your password is never typed on screen

The password prompt does not echo — not even dots. It is stored in ~/.config/shokz-auto-fill/config at mode 600 (only your user can read it), and it is never printed, never logged, and never written into the installer's log file. Requests to Navidrome use a salted token instead.

It never deletes your music

Clean-up only touches the numbered folders this tool created inside auto-list/, and only the ones past the newest two. If it cannot download anything at all, it aborts and leaves the drive exactly as it found it.

You can read every line first

The installer, the fill script and the test suite are all in the public repository. Nothing is downloaded at install time, and nothing in the repo is obfuscated or minified.

All questions
What even is a .command file?

It is a plain text file full of shell commands that macOS opens in Terminal when you double-click it. The .command extension is the only thing that makes Finder run it instead of showing it to you in a text editor. You can open install.command in TextEdit and read every line before you run it — and you probably should.

More on .command files →

Why should I trust a script from the internet with my password?

You shouldn't, on the strength of a web page. You should trust it because you read it. It is about 700 lines of commented shell across four files, it is not minified, it has no network calls except to the Navidrome server you name yourself, and it installs nothing except a Homebrew bash and jq.

If you would rather not hand over your real password, make a second Navidrome account for this and use that. It only ever needs permission to read your library.

The full trust answer →

Does anything get sent anywhere?

No. Two things are contacted: your Navidrome server, to fetch songs, and the USB drive in front of you, to write them. There is no phone-home, no crash reporting, no usage analytics and no auto-update.

Privacy in detail →

What happens if my server is down when I plug in?

It tries each URL you configured, in order. If none of them answer it aborts, writes the reason to its log, and leaves the existing music on the player untouched. It will not create an empty folder and it will not clear out your old lists.
