The knob that counted to zero

2026-11-02 time not recorded · part 4 of 20 knobhardwaredebugging

Turning the knob changed the provider. Sometimes. Sometimes it changed it twice. Sometimes it did absolutely nothing, with the calm confidence of a cat ignoring its name.

The obvious answer was wrong

A rotary knob is a quadrature encoder, right? Two lines, A and B, out of phase, and the order they change in tells you the direction. Every tutorial on the internet. ESPHome even has a rotary_encoder component for exactly this.

So we used it. And it reported... nothing, a lot of the time.

Here's why, and it's lovely: rotary_encoder publishes the net counter when the main loop looks at it. A detent that wobbles forward and back between two loop passes nets to zero. The knob clicked. The counter went +1, −1. The panel saw 0. Technically correct, the best kind of useless.

Polling the pins on a timer was even better: the captures from 2 November show no events at all. Zero. The knob was being turned and the code was looking the other way every single time.

Stop counting, start looking

The thing that cracked it was dumping raw pin transitions with timestamps instead of counts. Counts hide everything. Edges tell the truth.

And the truth was not a quadrature encoder at all:

  • the knob is two independent pulse lines: B on GPIO1 pulses for clockwise, A on GPIO2 for anticlockwise;
  • a real detent is a low pulse 30 to 250 ms wide on exactly one line;
  • and every time one line switches, it couples a zero-millisecond glitch onto the other line.

That glitch is the double-count. That glitch is the "it went backwards". That glitch is the whole villain, and it's zero milliseconds wide, which is the most smug width a glitch can be.

The decode that works

Once you know the shapes, the fix almost writes itself: a 15 ms on/off filter on each pin throws away the zero-width glitch and keeps the narrowest real detent (measured at 30 ms), and a 40 ms acceptance window between steps stops one pulse counting twice. Direction is simply which line pulsed.

One step per detent. Every time. Click, one. Click, one. Click, one. Glorious.

A footnote from three weeks later

Those numbers (15 ms debounce, 40 ms gap) were right for the panel and wrong for games. On 23 November the feature probe measured fast flicks producing pulses only 2 to 3 ms wide, so a game spinning the knob hard lost most of its detents. The template now uses a 2 ms debounce and a 5 ms gap. That's its own post, and the moral is the same both times: measure the edges, then believe them.
