Everything except the schematic was a rumour

2026-11-01 19:45 · part 1 of 17 hardwareidentification

The listing said 320x320. The panel is 360x360. Forty pixels. Tiny lie. Enormous consequences, because a round display addressed as a square of the wrong size shows you a cropped picture and no error message whatsoever. I found out by closing the shop page and opening a PDF, which is the single best decision of this entire project.

This is the post about the hour before any code. Spoiler: it was the most valuable hour of the build, and I spent it reading.

A specification written by a reseller is a rumour

The product page offered a resolution, a screen size, a processor family and a list of colour variants. It was wrong about the resolution, vague about the memory, and silent about three parts of the board that later mattered a great deal: a touch controller, a haptic driver, and the fact that the two share a bus.

What it did get right was the broad shape: an ESP32-S3, a round screen, a knob, a speaker output. Everything below that level of description was, charitably, vibes.

The pack the seller supplied was 290MB and mostly noise. Inside were Windows flashing utilities, a duplicate of the panel vendor's own IDE, several copies of the same example projects, and one folder of documentation. Four files in that folder answered every question I had over the next two days:

  • the panel initialisation sequence, which states the resolution and the bus width
  • a pin configuration header, which names every GPIO the board uses
  • an ESP-IDF demo, which shows the panel and touch being brought up for real
  • a schematic PDF, whose text layer names every net on the board

The pack is a mess, but it is the manufacturer's own mess, and it outranks a listing written to sell the thing. Four files out of 290MB. I'll take it.

What the board said about itself

Before trusting any document I asked the hardware. esptool over USB reported an ESP32-S3 in a QFN56 package, revision v0.2, with 16MB of flash and 8MB of PSRAM.

That matched the N16R8 naming in the listing, which is the one place the listing and reality agreed. It also told me something the listing did not: the module is an R8 variant, which means 8MB of PSRAM on the octal interface rather than the quad one. That distinction bites later, because a PSRAM configured as octal on a quad part does not work, and the failure is not obvious.

The panel's own initialisation sequence gave the resolution: 360 by 360, sixteen bits per pixel, over four QSPI data lines. The demo's pin header confirmed the bus: clock on 11, chip select on 12, data 0 through 3 on 13, 14, 15 and 16, reset on 17, backlight on 21.

So the first reliable facts were: a bigger screen than advertised (bonus pixels!), octal PSRAM, and a QSPI panel with a pinout that is fixed and documented. Already ahead.

The parts nobody documented

The schematic is where the board stops being a generic dev module. Its net names are a list of the components somebody actually placed:

  • TP0SCL and TP0SDA, the touch controller on the shared I2C bus
  • LCD0QSPI0SCL and the four data lines, the panel bus
  • HAPTIC0SCL, HAPTIC0SDA, HAPTIC0EN and HAPTIC0TRIG, a haptic motor driver
  • LEDA and LEDK, the backlight rails

I had already noticed two devices on the I2C bus before reading any of this, at 0x15 and 0x5A, and had assumed the second was something mundane and ignorable. It was not. Oh, it was not. The schematic named it: a DRV2605LDGSR, a haptic driver with an effect library, sitting on the same bus as the touchscreen. The board could buzz, and nobody had told me.

Two more findings from the same list. There is no ambient light sensor anywhere in it, so the board cannot measure how dark the room is, whatever it might feel like it should do. And the haptic driver's enable and trigger nets go to capacitors and a test pad rather than to any GPIO, which means the motor is enabled in hardware and driven entirely over I2C. That single observation saved me from hunting for a pin that does not exist.

The vendor demos never mention the haptic driver. Neither the Arduino demo nor the IDF demo references it, which is why the address looked like an unexplained curiosity for most of a day.

Reading a schematic without being able to read the labels

The PDF is in Chinese, and the useful content is not in the prose anyway. It is in the text layer, which contains the netlist: lines that pair a net label with the pins it touches.

I extracted it with pypdf and grepped for the nets I cared about. That is how HAPTIC0EN was shown to connect to capacitors rather than to a GPIO, and how the part number for the driver surfaced at all. A schematic is a graph, and a graph survives translation. I can't read the labels. I can read the wires. Good enough.

The same technique produced the pin table I later used as the single source of truth for the firmware. Clock on 11, chip select on 12, data lines on 13 to 16, reset on 17, backlight on 21, touch on 9 and 10 with interrupt on 7 and reset on 8, haptics on the same bus, knob on 1 and 2, audio on 3, 45 and 42 with a mute line on 46.

The address that answered when it should not have

The first firmware did nothing but scan the I2C bus and print what answered, which is the cheapest possible census of a new board.

Two devices replied. 0x15 is the CST816 touch controller, which the vendor's pin header names directly. 0x5A is not named in any of the demo sources, and does not appear in the pin configuration header, and does not answer to any of the usual small peripherals I tried against it.

That is the sort of loose end it is tempting to leave. The touchscreen worked, the screen worked, and an unexplained address on a shared bus is harmless until something else tries to use it. I wrote it down as a question and moved on, which was the right call at the time and the wrong call for about a day, because the answer changes what the device is.

The schematic answered it later: the address is a DRV2605LDGSR haptic driver, the part that makes the knob click and the alerts buzz. A board with a haptic motor is a different device from a board without one, and I had been treating this one as a screen with a knob. It was a screen with a knob and opinions about touch.

Two habits came out of that. A bus scan belongs in the first firmware, not the last, because it costs nothing and it tells you what you actually have. And an unexplained address is a question to keep, not a curiosity to dismiss: it is a component somebody paid for and wired up, and it is probably there to do something you will want.

What this changed about how I start

I have started other hardware projects by writing a sketch and seeing what happens. That works when the hardware is a known quantity and fails when the documentation is a reseller's summary.

The order that worked here was: ask the chip what it is, read the manufacturer's pin list, extract the schematic's net names, write the resulting table into a note, and only then write firmware. It took an hour, and it prevented at least three bugs whose symptoms would have pointed somewhere else entirely: a cropped screen, a PSRAM mismatch, and a hunt for a haptic enable pin that is wired high on purpose.

The habit worth keeping is not "read the manual". It is narrower and more useful: find the document the manufacturer used to build the thing, and treat every other description of it as a summary that may have been written by someone who never opened it.

The listing is a summary. The pack is the source. The schematic is the truth.

Nuts, the lot of it. Wonderful.
